# Applied AI Automation Company Handbook

## Purpose of This Guide

This handbook defines how the company should position, sell, design, build, deploy, and support custom AI systems for businesses.

The company’s role is not simply to provide access to artificial intelligence models. Its role is to study how a business operates, understand its workflows, connect its systems, organise its knowledge, and build secure AI agents that can complete real work across departments.

This guide should be used internally when:

- explaining the company to clients;
- qualifying leads;
- running discovery meetings;
- designing solutions;
- pricing projects;
- planning pilots;
- building knowledge systems;
- deciding between local, cloud, and hybrid deployment;
- managing risk;
- defining support and maintenance.

---

# 1. Company Vision

The company builds custom AI systems that automate business workflows across departments.

Each system is designed around the client’s:

- operations;
- data;
- employees;
- tools;
- approval processes;
- security requirements;
- business rules;
- infrastructure.

The solution may include AI agents, custom models, knowledge bases, knowledge graphs, integrations, interfaces, workflow orchestration, and private infrastructure.

The final result should not feel like a generic chatbot. It should feel like an intelligent operational layer built specifically for the company.

## Core Vision Statement

> We build the private intelligence and automation infrastructure of companies, from their knowledge base and knowledge graph to the AI agents that use them to perform work across connected systems.

## Simple Client-Facing Version

> We study how your company works, identify what can be automated, and build secure AI agents that perform those workflows across your existing systems.

# 2. What the Company Actually Sells

The company does not sell models.

The company sells operational outcomes.

A client may already have access to ChatGPT, Claude, Codex, Gemini, or another general-purpose AI tool. Access to a model is not the main problem.

The real problems are usually:

- company knowledge is scattered;
- workflows are undocumented;
- employees depend on manual work;
- systems do not communicate with each other;
- data is difficult to access;
- approvals happen through email or messaging;
- employees repeat the same tasks every day;
- the company has no safe way to let AI take action;
- management cannot measure AI performance;
- generic AI tools do not understand company-specific rules.

The company solves these problems by delivering a complete system around the AI model.

That system may include:

1. workflow discovery;
2. process redesign;
3. knowledge organisation;
4. system integrations;
5. agent design;
6. custom interfaces;
7. permissions and approvals;
8. model routing;
9. deployment;
10. testing;
11. monitoring;
12. continuous improvement.

# 3. The Main Differentiation

## 3.1 The Company Studies the Business First

The company does not begin by asking:

> Which AI model do you want?

It begins by asking:

> How does your team currently complete this work?

The first responsibility is to understand the client’s real operation.

This includes:

- meeting employees and managers;
- observing tasks;
- mapping each workflow;
- identifying the systems used;
- understanding business rules;
- documenting approval chains;
- measuring time and cost;
- reviewing common mistakes;
- identifying exceptions;
- deciding which tasks should remain human-controlled.

This is one of the strongest differences between the company and generic AI tools.

## 3.2 The Agent Performs Work

A general AI assistant often answers questions or generates content.

A custom operational agent can:

- read information;
- reason about the situation;
- search company knowledge;
- retrieve data;
- create records;
- update systems;
- prepare documents;
- send messages;
- request approval;
- monitor progress;
- escalate exceptions;
- generate reports;
- trigger another workflow.

The company should sell completed work, not conversations.

## 3.3 The System Connects to Existing Tools

The agent should work with the systems the client already uses.

Examples include:

- ERP platforms;
- CRM platforms;
- accounting systems;
- email;
- calendars;
- Slack;
- Microsoft Teams;
- WhatsApp;
- cloud storage;
- internal databases;
- spreadsheets;
- HR systems;
- customer-support systems;
- project-management platforms;
- social-media tools;
- proprietary software;
- external APIs.

The integration layer is part of the core value.

## 3.4 The System Follows Company Rules

A custom AI system should understand:

- approval hierarchies;
- department responsibilities;
- pricing rules;
- client categories;
- document templates;
- brand guidelines;
- escalation procedures;
- prohibited actions;
- data-access policies;
- security boundaries;
- acceptable confidence levels;
- regulatory requirements.

The goal is not to make the agent unrestricted.

The goal is to make it useful, controlled, traceable, and reliable.

## 3.5 The System Can Use Multiple Models

Different tasks may require different models.

A strong architecture may use:

- a small local model for classification;
- a vision model for invoices or images;
- a strong reasoning model for complex decisions;
- a speech model for calls;
- an image model for creative work;
- deterministic code for calculations;
- a retrieval system for company knowledge;
- a human approval step for sensitive actions.

The system should automatically select the most suitable model for each task.

This can improve:

- cost;
- speed;
- privacy;
- reliability;
- output quality.

## 3.6 Flexible Deployment

The company should support three main deployment models.

### Local or On-Premise

The system operates on infrastructure owned or controlled by the client.

This may be appropriate when:

- data must remain inside the organisation;
- internet access is restricted;
- the client has strict security requirements;
- the client requires local inference;
- the workload is stable enough to justify dedicated hardware.

### Private Cloud

The system is deployed in a dedicated cloud environment for the client.

This may offer:

- easier scaling;
- remote access;
- managed infrastructure;
- stronger availability;
- simpler updates.

### Hybrid

Sensitive workloads remain local while approved workloads use cloud models or services.

This may be the best balance for many organisations.

Local deployment should never be described as automatically secure.

Security depends on:

- architecture;
- access control;
- encryption;
- network configuration;
- monitoring;
- patching;
- governance;
- operational discipline.

## 3.7 Governance and Accountability

Every serious AI system should include controls.

Important actions should have:

- user permissions;
- execution limits;
- approval requirements;
- audit logs;
- source references;
- model and prompt versioning;
- cost tracking;
- failure handling;
- human escalation;
- rollback or correction procedures.

A strong client message is:

> Every important action is controlled, traceable, and reviewable.

## 3.8 Ongoing Responsibility

The company should not disappear after deployment.

Its ongoing responsibilities may include:

- monitoring;
- support;
- optimisation;
- integration maintenance;
- model updates;
- knowledge updates;
- workflow changes;
- performance reporting;
- security improvements;
- incident investigation;
- employee training.

This makes the company a long-term operational partner rather than a one-time development agency.

# 6. Choosing the Right Technical Solution

The company should not use AI where ordinary software is more reliable.

Use the following guide.

## Use a Knowledge Base When

- the client needs document search;
- employees need internal answers;
- content must be summarised;
- policies must be retrieved;
- responses need source references.

## Use a Knowledge Graph When

- relationships are important;
- multiple systems must be connected;
- dependencies must be tracked;
- complex entity-based questions must be answered;
- the client needs graph reasoning.

## Use Databases and APIs When

- information is structured;
- records must be updated;
- real-time system data is required;
- deterministic accuracy is necessary.

## Use Fine-Tuning or Custom Models When

- specialised classification is required;
- output style must be highly consistent;
- there are enough quality examples;
- local inference is required;
- domain-specific language matters;
- forecasting or anomaly detection is needed.

## Use Agents When

- the task requires multiple steps;
- different tools are involved;
- the system must make decisions;
- the workflow includes exceptions;
- actions must be executed.

## Use Traditional Automation When

- rules are fully deterministic;
- no reasoning is required;
- the task is simple and repetitive;
- reliability is more important than flexibility.

The company’s responsibility is to choose the simplest reliable architecture.

# 11. How to Explain the Company to Clients

Do not begin with model names, token usage, vector databases, or agent frameworks.

Begin with the client’s workflow.

Ask:

- What repetitive process takes the most time?
- Who starts it?
- Which systems are involved?
- What information enters the process?
- What decisions are made?
- Which actions require approval?
- What happens when something goes wrong?
- How often does it occur?
- How long does it take?
- What errors usually happen?
- What does delay cost?

Then explain the solution in operational language.

Example:

> We can build a secure system that handles the repetitive parts of this process, works across your existing tools, and sends only important decisions or exceptions to your team.

## Client-Facing Company Description

> We help companies automate repetitive operational work using custom AI agents. We begin by studying how the team currently works, including the systems, documents, approvals, and decisions involved. We then build an AI system that can perform the workflow across existing tools, with the required permissions, controls, and human approvals. The system can run locally, in a private cloud, or through a hybrid architecture.

# 14. Human-in-the-Loop Design

Agent actions should be classified by risk.

## Low Risk

The agent may execute automatically.

Examples:

- summarising documents;
- classifying emails;
- generating internal reports;
- preparing drafts.

## Medium Risk

The agent may execute and notify.

Examples:

- updating CRM records;
- sending routine internal messages;
- scheduling standard tasks.

## High Risk

The agent prepares the action but requires approval.

Examples:

- payments;
- legal communication;
- publishing sensitive content;
- deleting records;
- changing contracts;
- sending important customer messages.

## Prohibited

The agent should never perform the action.

Examples depend on the client, regulation, and business context.

Each project should document these levels.

# 24. What the Company Should Avoid

The company should avoid:

- promising that an agent can do anything;
- selling only a chatbot;
- claiming local deployment is automatically secure;
- training a model when retrieval or automation is enough;
- using AI for deterministic tasks that normal code can solve;
- offering unlimited usage without controls;
- starting with the entire company;
- building without measurable success criteria;
- allowing sensitive actions without approval;
- delivering without monitoring;
- depending only on one-time project revenue;
- pricing only by development hours;
- ignoring employee adoption;
- using technical language before understanding the business problem.

# 25. Core Operating Principles

## Principle 1: Understand Before Building

No serious system should be designed before the workflow is understood.

## Principle 2: Sell Outcomes

Clients care about:

- time saved;
- errors reduced;
- speed improved;
- cost reduced;
- capacity increased;
- control improved.

## Principle 3: Use the Simplest Reliable Architecture

Not every problem needs an agent, model, graph, or LLM.

## Principle 4: Keep Humans in Control

Sensitive decisions should remain reviewable and accountable.

## Principle 5: Design for Production

Every system should include testing, monitoring, logs, permissions, and failure handling.

## Principle 6: Build for Expansion

A successful pilot should create a foundation for more workflows and departments.

## Principle 7: Maintain Long-Term Responsibility

The value continues after deployment through support, optimisation, and governance.
